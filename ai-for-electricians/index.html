AI tools for electricians: what carries over from an HVAC shop

AI tools for electricians: what an HVAC owner has seen work on calls, follow-up and paperwork, what to pilot on panel and EV charger estimates, and what to skip.

Blake RuwaliOwner of Home+ Air & Heat and founder of Techeo5 min read · Nov 14, 2026

AI tools for electricians are most useful on the work around the trade: answering calls the office can't pick up, following up on unsold estimates, and drafting permit and job paperwork. A photo-based estimate for panels, EV chargers and generators is worth a small pilot, but it is a draft that a qualified electrician reviews every time. The hands-on work, and every decision about code and safety, stays with your electricians.

I should be upfront. I run an HVAC company, not an electrical one. I can't tell you how to size a service or what a code requires, and I won't try. What I can tell you is what I've seen work in a trades shop, because a lot of it carries over. The wider picture is in what AI actually does for the trades.

What carries over from HVAC

Electrical and HVAC shops have the same office problems. Calls come in while everyone is on a job. Estimates go out and nobody chases them. Paperwork piles up after the work is done. Those problems are not specific to a trade, and neither are the fixes.

Calls

When a customer's power is out or a breaker keeps tripping, they call the next company if nobody answers. At Home+ we use an AI phone agent for calls the office can't pick up. It collects the problem, the address and a callback number, and puts the job into our system for a person to follow up. It does not diagnose, quote or promise arrival times. The setup is in how to answer every missed call with AI, and there's a short checklist at /missed-call-fix/.

For an electrical shop I'd add one rule up front: anything that sounds like a safety problem, such as burning smells, sparking or a shocked person, goes straight to a human or to emergency services. The AI should not try to handle it.

Follow-up

Most unsold estimates die from silence, not from a "no." A message on day 2 and another on day 7, written in your voice, that stops when the customer replies, is the same in every trade. I describe the cadence in AI follow-up for unsold estimates.

Paperwork

Permit applications, inspection scheduling notes, job summaries and customer handoff sheets are mostly writing from facts you already have. AI can draft them from your notes. A person checks every line before it goes to a customer or an inspector, because the paperwork is the record.

What I'd pilot, carefully: photo-based estimates

At Home+ our estimate copilot reads a tech's photos and drafts a proposal using our own price book. A person checks every one. The AI never invents a price because it only picks from lines we have already set. I explain how in AI estimates with a human check.

Could the same idea work for panel upgrades, EV charger installs or generators? It might. The photos you'd want are the panel, the meter, the nameplate and the run to where the equipment goes. I haven't built this for electrical work, so treat it as a pilot, not a promise:

  1. Pick one job type you quote often.
  2. Have an electrician take the photos the way they would for their own estimate.
  3. Let the tool draft the line items from your price book.
  4. Have a qualified electrician review every draft, and write down every correction.
  5. After 20 or 30 drafts, see whether the corrections are small or constant.

If the corrections are constant, the tool isn't ready, or your price book isn't. A photo can't show everything behind a wall, and an electrician decides what the job needs. The estimate is only a first draft of the paperwork.

What's different from HVAC

  • The stakes in the walls. Wiring and panel work carry fire and shock risk. I'd keep the AI further from anything technical than I do in HVAC.
  • Code and inspections. Requirements vary by place and change over time. Don't ask a chatbot what the code says. Ask your inspector, the authority having jurisdiction, or the code book. Use AI to help you write the question, not to answer it.
  • Load decisions. In my shop a person owns equipment sizing, and I treat a scan or a photo as input, not as the answer. Your version of that is service size and circuit capacity. A qualified electrician decides.
  • Your price book. If it is messy or out of date, a photo-based estimate will be messy too. Clean it first.

What I'd skip

  • Anything that lets AI make a safety call or state a code requirement to a customer.
  • Quoting prices from a general chatbot. It doesn't know your labor rate or your supplier costs.
  • Buying a big package before you've counted your missed calls and your unfollowed estimates.

Where I'd start

  1. Count your unanswered calls for two weeks.
  2. Write one follow-up rule for unsold estimates.
  3. Only then try a photo-based estimate pilot on a single job type.

If you're talking to vendors, use the questions in how I'd pick an AI partner, and ask any of them to show a real call or estimate becoming a real record in your system. For prompts you can use today without connecting anything, see what ChatGPT is good for.

FAQ

What AI tools can electricians use right now? Phone agents for missed calls, follow-up campaigns for unsold estimates, and writing help for job summaries and customer messages. Photo-based estimating is worth a small pilot. Each one needs a person checking anything about price, scope or safety.

Can AI write an electrical estimate from photos? It can draft one from photos and your own price book, but I haven't built that for electrical work, so I'd pilot it. A qualified electrician should review every draft, because a photo can't show what's behind a wall or what the job really needs.

Can I ask ChatGPT about electrical code? I wouldn't rely on it. Code requirements vary by location and change over time, and a confident answer can still be wrong. Use your local authority or the code book, and let a qualified electrician decide.

Should AI answer the phone for an electrical company? It can for calls the office can't pick up, collecting details and creating a job for a person to follow up. Safety situations like sparking, burning smells or injuries should go to a human or emergency services right away.

Does this replace an electrician? No. It takes some of the phone and paperwork load off the office. The work in the panel stays with a person who is trained and qualified to do it.
