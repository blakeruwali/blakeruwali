How much does AI cost for a small HVAC company? The cost structure

How much does AI cost for a small HVAC company? How the costs are structured, how to compare vendors, how to run a small paid pilot, and what to ask.

Blake RuwaliOwner of Home+ Air & Heat and founder of Techeo5 min read · Nov 14, 2026

How much AI costs for a small HVAC company depends less on one price and more on how the price is built. Most tools charge in a few different ways at once: per minute or per call for phones, per user for software, a setup or implementation fee, usage costs for the AI model underneath, and your own time to review the work. Once you know the pieces, you can compare vendors fairly and test with a small pilot before you commit.

I'm not going to quote prices here. They change, they depend on your volume, and any number I gave you would be out of date. What I can do is show you how to read a quote. I run Home+ Air & Heat and build the AI we use, so I've seen the cost from both sides.

The five cost pieces

1. Per-minute or per-call pricing (phone agents). A phone agent usually bills for the time it spends on calls, or per call handled. Your cost moves with how many calls come in and how long they run. A busy month costs more than a slow one. Ask what counts as a minute: ringing, hold, transfers, spam calls.

2. Per-seat pricing (software). Many tools charge per user. Count who actually needs a login: the office, the estimators, the owner. Techs might not. Ask what happens when you add or remove a seat mid-term.

3. Setup or implementation fees. Connecting the tool to your schedule, price book and phone numbers takes work. Some vendors charge once, some roll it into the monthly cost, some do it for free. Find out what is included, who does the work, and how long it takes.

4. Usage-based AI model costs. Under the hood, tools call AI models that bill by usage. Some vendors absorb this. Some pass it through. If a tool bills on usage, ask how you can see it and whether you can set a cap.

5. Your time and your office's time. This is the one nobody quotes. Someone has to review calls, check drafts, correct mistakes and answer questions. A tool that needs an hour a day of review costs more than its invoice says. I plan for that time at the start and expect it to shrink as the tool earns trust.

How to compare two quotes

Put them side by side and answer the same questions for each:

  1. What do I pay if volume is low? What if it doubles?
  2. What is billed monthly, what is billed once, and what is billed by usage?
  3. What is included in setup, and who does it?
  4. What does the tool connect to, and do I pay for those connections?
  5. How much of my time will it take each week?
  6. What happens to the cost if I stop using it?

If a vendor can't answer these plainly, that tells you something.

Be careful with the "cost versus return" conversation

Vendors love to show a return on investment. I won't give you one, because it depends entirely on your shop: your call volume, your close rate, your average job. If anyone hands you a return figure without looking at your numbers, treat it as marketing. Start with what you can count yourself, like how many calls went unanswered last week, and see what a pilot actually changes.

How to run a small paid pilot

A pilot is a short, paid, limited test. It protects you from signing a long contract for something you haven't seen work.

  1. Pick one workflow. For most shops that is missed calls. See how to answer every missed call and the missed-call checklist.
  2. Write down the baseline first. How many calls went unanswered last week and what happened to them.
  3. Set the length. A few weeks is enough to see real calls, not a demo.
  4. Define what success looks like before you start. Something you can count, written down.
  5. Set the guardrails. What the tool may say, what it may never say, and when it hands off to a person.
  6. Keep a person reviewing. Read the calls, check the drafts. Anything touching money, scope or safety gets a human decision.
  7. Write down what failed. The failures tell you more than the wins.
  8. Decide at the end, not before. Continue, change or stop.

What to ask about the contract

  • Length. Month to month, or a year? What does it cost to leave early?
  • Auto-renewal. How and when do I cancel?
  • Price changes. Can the price change during the term?
  • Data export. If I leave, can I take my call recordings, transcripts, customer records and settings? In what format?
  • Data ownership. Who owns what the tool collects about my customers?
  • Training on my data. Is my customers' information used to train anything?
  • Support. Who do I call when it breaks, and when do they answer?

The export question matters most to me. If your data goes into a tool and can't come back out, you are renting your own business. More questions are in questions to ask an AI partner.

Don't forget what's free

ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini all offer plans you can start with, and they're useful for writing and admin. Check each provider's current plans for what they offer. Just remember that a general chatbot doesn't know your schedule or price book. See ChatGPT for contractors for what it's good for and where it goes wrong.

Where to start

Pick one leak. Write down the baseline. Ask for a quote that spells out all five pieces. Run a short paid pilot. For a map of tools by job, see AI tools for HVAC owners, and for the big picture, AI for the trades.

FAQ

How much does AI cost for a small HVAC company? It varies by tool and volume, and I won't give a number that could mislead you. Costs usually come from per-minute or per-call fees, per-seat fees, setup fees, usage-based AI charges and the time your office spends reviewing.

Is a per-minute phone agent cheaper than a per-seat tool? Neither is cheaper in general. Per-minute cost follows your call volume, per-seat cost follows your headcount. Compare them against your own numbers.

What is a paid pilot? A short, limited, paid test of one workflow with clear success criteria. It lets you see real results in your shop before you sign a longer contract.

What should I ask about contracts? Length, auto-renewal, price changes, data export and ownership, whether your customer data trains anything, and what support looks like.

Do I need to hire someone to run AI tools? Not necessarily, but someone must own the review. A person in the office should check the output, especially anything touching money, scope or safety.
