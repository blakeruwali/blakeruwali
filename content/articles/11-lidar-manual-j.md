# Manual J load calculation with iPad LiDAR: how the scan feeds the numbers

**Meta description:** How a Manual J load calculation works with iPad LiDAR: scan to floor plan to load calc, what the scan gets right, and what a person must still check.
**Suggested URL:** /lidar-manual-j/
**Byline:** Blake Ruwali, owner of Home+ Air & Heat and founder of Techeo

A Manual J load calculation with iPad LiDAR works in three steps: you scan the house with the iPad, the scan becomes a floor plan with measured rooms, and those measurements feed the load calculation. The scan replaces tape-measure work and guessing at square footage. It does not replace a person, because insulation, windows, duct location and air leakage still have to be entered or confirmed by someone who has looked at the house.

I'm Blake. I run Home+ Air & Heat on Long Island and in New York City, and we use this process at Home+. Here is how it works and where I don't trust it blindly.

## Why measure at all

Sizing equipment by rule of thumb means picking a number of square feet per ton and moving on. It ignores the shape of the house, how much glass it has, which way it faces and what is above and below each room. Two houses with the same square footage can need very different equipment.

A Manual J load calculation is the standard way to estimate how much heating and cooling a house needs. It is only as good as the inputs. If the dimensions are wrong, everything downstream is wrong. Measuring the house properly is the first input, and it is the one a scan is good at.

## How the flow works

1. **A tech scans the house.** Walking room by room with an iPad that has a LiDAR sensor. The sensor measures distance to walls, floors and ceilings.
2. **The scan becomes a floor plan.** Rooms, wall lengths, ceiling heights and openings show up as a plan with real dimensions.
3. **A person corrects the plan.** Walls that were missed, a room that merged with another, a closet that wasn't scanned. Fix these before anything else.
4. **The measurements go into the load calculation.** Areas of walls, floors, ceilings and glazing come from the plan instead of being typed in by hand.
5. **A person enters and checks the rest.** More on this below.
6. **A person reviews the result** before it affects any equipment recommendation or price.

I'm deliberately not naming the load calculation software here. The method matters more than the brand, and you should check what any tool you use is built on.

## What the scan gets right

- **Room dimensions.** Length, width and height, taken the same way every time instead of by whoever held the tape.
- **Floor area by level.** Useful for finished space versus basement or attic.
- **Wall and ceiling areas.** These come straight from the geometry.
- **Time.** It takes a lot less typing than building a house plan from scratch.

## What a person still has to enter or check

This is the part that matters. The scan measures space. It does not know what is inside the walls.

- **Insulation.** In walls, attics, floors over crawlspaces and basements. A scan can't see it. Someone has to look, ask or make a reasonable assumption and note it.
- **Windows and doors.** Size is measurable. Type, glass, frames and condition are not.
- **Duct location.** Ducts in a conditioned basement behave very differently from ducts in a hot attic. This changes the result and the scan won't tell you.
- **Air leakage.** How tight the house is has to be assumed or tested. I treat that assumption as something to write down, not something to hide inside a default.
- **Orientation and shading.** Which way the house faces, trees, neighboring buildings.
- **Occupancy and equipment.** Anything unusual that adds load.
- **Scan errors.** Mirrors, big windows and cluttered rooms can confuse the sensor. Compare the plan against the house.

## Where it goes wrong

- A room is missing or doubled and nobody notices because the total looks reasonable.
- Ceiling heights are wrong in rooms with vaulted or sloped ceilings.
- The default insulation or leakage value is accepted because the field was pre-filled.
- The house was changed (an addition, a finished attic) and the plan reflects only part of it.

Each of these is a person problem, not a sensor problem. That is why a person reviews every calculation.

## Why this matters for the customer

Equipment sized to a measured load, with the inputs written down, gives you something you can explain. If a customer asks why we recommended a certain size, there is a plan and a set of inputs behind the answer. Equipment size is a decision about a customer's money and comfort, so a person owns it, not the software.

I'm not making claims about savings or results from this. What I will say is that I'd rather defend a number built from measurements than one built from a rule of thumb.

## Where to start

Scan three houses you have already sized by hand. Run the load calculation. Compare it to what you installed or quoted, and write down every difference and why. Do that before you let it change how you size anything.

## FAQ

**Can an iPad do a Manual J load calculation?**
An iPad with a LiDAR sensor can scan the house and produce a floor plan with measured dimensions. Those measurements then feed a load calculation. The iPad doesn't decide the answer, and someone still has to enter and review the inputs.

**Is LiDAR measurement accurate enough for sizing?**
For room dimensions it is a solid starting point, but it can miss walls, misread glass or struggle in cluttered rooms. A person should compare the floor plan to the house and correct it before using it.

**What can't the scan tell you?**
Insulation levels, window type and condition, where ducts run, and how leaky the house is. Those have to be observed, asked about or assumed and written down.

**Why not just size by square footage?**
Square footage ignores glass, orientation, ceiling heights, insulation and duct location. Houses with the same floor area can have different loads, so measuring and calculating beats a rule of thumb.

**Does a load calculation replace a site visit?**
No. The scan happens in the house, and the inputs that matter most come from someone looking at the house. A person approves the result before it drives any recommendation.
